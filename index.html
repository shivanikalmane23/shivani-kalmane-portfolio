About

Leadership

Leading without owning a single headcount.

A product manager rarely has direct authority over the people who build the product. No reporting line to engineering, no formal say over design. Just a roadmap and a point of view. The job is convincing people to follow it anyway.

"Product managers lead without formal authority, creating alignment through trust, influence, and a shared vision, not a title."

At Schneider Electric, that meant sitting between developers who owned the how and executives who owned the why, and finding language both could act on. It also meant showing up consistently outside my own roadmap: as a core team member of Schneider Electric's Women in Digital Leadership initiative, building programs that gave other women in tech room to lead, not just participate.

The way I naturally lead is through influence, which sounds obvious for a product manager. In practice it means fewer status decks and more one-on-one conversations, because that's where trust actually gets built.

She Sparks, 2025. Organizing our first She Sparks event, where women from across the team pitched their ideas directly to Schneider Electric's leaders.
Innovation

Innovation is a culture question before it's a tools question.

01

Permission to fail

It's difficult to innovate when people are afraid to fail. Building an environment where teams can experiment, challenge assumptions, and learn from what doesn't work matters as much as the tools you give them.

02

A cloud infrastructure visit, Zurich

What stood out wasn't the scale of the infrastructure. It was the mindset behind it: building for continuous evolution, not a single launch.

03

An ecosystem visit, Singapore

Technology as an ecosystem enabler. The most impactful tools weren't the most sophisticated ones. They were defined by the problems they solved.

Product strategy work has taught me the same lesson twice: the constraint on innovation is almost never a lack of ideas. It's whether the team trusts that surfacing a bad idea early is safer than shipping it quietly.

That's the thread running through an MBA elective on innovation mindset, a factory floor in Singapore, and a data centre in Zurich. The organizations that actually innovate are the ones that built a culture for it first, then went looking for the technology.

Entrepreneurship project, IMD. Pitching our team's retrofit device: a concept to turn a regular fridge into a smart one that tells you what you can cook with what's already inside. Innovation starting from a willingness to prototype the weird idea.

AI & Product Management

Vibe coding changed what "product manager" means to me.

Product management used to stop at the PRD. A designer would mock it up, an engineer would build it, and weeks later a PM would find out if the idea actually held up.

That gap is closing. With AI-assisted, "vibe-coded" prototypes, I can go from a rough idea to a clickable, working version in an afternoon, before a single engineering hour is spent on it. For IMD's Future Lab, I used AI-assisted development to build a working prototype of an AI-powered talent marketplace myself, rather than only writing a spec and handing it off.

"Don't outsource your thinking to AI. Use it to amplify your impact."

An engineer, at an IMD session on Agentic AI

That's the distinction I try to hold onto. Vibe coding isn't a shortcut around product thinking. It's a faster feedback loop for it. The skill is shifting from "can I describe this well enough for someone else to build" to "can I build enough of this myself to know if it's worth building at all."

How I Work

Five ways I show up on a team.

The traits I lean on hardest, in the order they show up, each one in service of leading without a title to back it up.

1
Futuristic
I default to picturing where the product could go, and use that picture to get buy-in before the roadmap slide even exists.
2
Communication
The unglamorous skill behind influence without authority: translating engineer to executive and back, in the same conversation.
3
Arranger
Give me a fast-changing roadmap, competing priorities, and a room full of opinions, and I'll find the arrangement that actually moves.
4
Includer
The best product decisions happen when the person closest to the problem is in the room. I make sure of it.
5
Positivity
The energy that keeps a team experimenting after the third failed prototype, not just after the first success.
